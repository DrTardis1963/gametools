KSP delta-v and TWR calculator

Game data: KSP 1.12.5 (stock)

Plan a rocket before you build it: how much delta-v each stage gives, whether it can lift off, and how long each burn takes.

How it works

Δv = Isp × 9.80665 × ln(m₀ ÷ m₁)
TWR = thrust ÷ (mass × surface gravity)

The rocket equation says how much a stage can change your speed. Isp is the engine's efficiency in seconds; multiplying it by 9.80665 (the constant KSP uses) turns it into exhaust speed in m/s. m₀ is the whole rocket's mass when the stage lights, m₁ its mass when that stage's tanks run dry. Only the ratio matters, so tonnes are fine.

Stages are listed in firing order. Each stage's own mass covers its tanks, engines, decoupler and anything else that is thrown away with it. While a stage burns it also carries every stage above it plus the payload, which is why the bottom stage has the hardest job.

Sea level or vacuum. Engines lose efficiency and thrust in air. Choose Kerbin sea level for a stage that lights on the launch pad and Vacuum for everything above about 25 km. A real ascent lands somewhere between the two figures for the first stage. In KSP an engine burns fuel at the same rate in any air pressure, so its thrust falls in step with its Isp.

TWR (thrust-to-weight ratio) above 1 means the stage can lift itself off that body. For a Kerbin launch, 1.2 to 1.6 at liftoff works well: lower wastes fuel fighting gravity, higher wastes it fighting drag. Upper stages can run far lower, since they push sideways in orbit rather than straight up.

Worked example: a two-stage Mun rocket

The form starts with this rocket. The payload is 1 t (a Mk1 pod, parachute and a few extras). The upper stage is a Terrier with an FL-T400 tank and a decoupler: 2.8 t full, 0.8 t empty. The lower stage is a Reliant with two FL-T800 tanks and a decoupler: 10.25 t full, 2.25 t empty.

Stagem₀ → m₁ (t)ΔvTWRBurn
1: Reliant, sea level14.05 → 6.052,190 m/s1.49101 s
2: Terrier, vacuum3.80 → 1.802,528 m/s1.61113 s

The first stage: 265 × 9.80665 × ln(14.05 ÷ 6.05) = 2,190 m/s at sea level, or 2,561 m/s if it were all in vacuum. Its sea-level thrust is 205.16 kN, so liftoff TWR is 205.16 ÷ (14.05 × 9.81) = 1.49, rising to 3.46 as the tanks empty. The Terrier then gives 345 × 9.80665 × ln(3.8 ÷ 1.8) = 2,528 m/s, for 4,718 m/s in total.

That is enough to reach low Kerbin orbit (about 3,400 m/s) with roughly 1,300 m/s left for the Mun: a transfer burn (about 860 m/s) and capture into a low orbit (about 310 m/s), but not a landing and return. Add a third stage or a bigger upper tank before you aim for the surface.

What this leaves out

  • Boosters burning alongside the core. This treats stages as firing one after another. For side boosters that burn together with a centre engine (including asparagus staging), the game's own staging readout in the VAB is more accurate.
  • Drag and gravity losses. Δv is what the fuel can do, not what survives a clumsy ascent. A steep or late gravity turn can cost hundreds of m/s.
  • Other air pressures. Eve's surface (5 atm) is far worse than Kerbin's sea level; Duna's thin air (0.07 atm) is close to vacuum. Only 1 atm and vacuum are offered here.
  • Mixed engines in one stage. Pick the engine that dominates, or work out a combined Isp with "custom".

Tips

  • Solid boosters fill in their own full and empty mass when you pick them; add the mass of any decouplers and nose cones.
  • The Nerv's 800 s Isp is huge in vacuum but it is heavy (3 t) and weak (60 kN). It pays off for big interplanetary stages, not small landers.
  • For a lander, set "TWR measured on" to the body you'll land on. Mun gravity is a sixth of Kerbin's, so a TWR of 2-3 there is easy.
